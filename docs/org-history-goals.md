# Organization history: why it exists

An organization’s work outlives any chat, project, person, or machine. This feature preserves **what happened, why it was chosen, who was responsible, what was ruled out, and what followed**—so people and agents can understand the organization without repeatedly reconstructing its past.

## Goals

1. **Durable organizational memory**  
   Keep decisions, reasons, and links usable through restarts, archived projects, staff changes, and restoration from the organization’s Git repo.

2. **Trustworthy provenance**  
   Distinguish recorded facts, attributed explanations, and unknowns. Never turn a plausible interpretation into historical truth.

3. **Accountable autonomy**  
   Make unattended work understandable: who initiated it, who decided, who executed, and what authority permitted it. An operator’s request is not blanket approval of every later choice.

4. **Remember paths not taken**  
   Preserve rejected, deferred, and deliberately excluded options—including their reasons, conditions, and later reversals—so settled questions aren’t endlessly reopened.

5. **Connect intent to outcomes**  
   Follow explicit links from goals and requests through evidence and decisions to implementation and observed results, including dependencies across projects.

6. **Cheap, targeted context**  
   Answer routine questions through small, cited records rather than expensive transcript-wide research. Give agents the relevant context without flooding their prompts.

7. **Make the work comprehensible**  
   Produce clear, informative timelines and linked views that let a newcomer understand a chain of work, with deeper evidence available when needed.

8. **Private, portable, recoverable history**  
   Respect access boundaries throughout retrieval and presentation. Keep canonical history in the organization’s Git workspace, while being honest that commits alone aren’t an off-device backup.

## Non-goals

This is **not** exhaustive surveillance, hidden model reasoning, a tamper-proof legal audit, a replacement for current state or requirements, or automatic public reporting.

## Guiding principle

**Preserve enough trustworthy context to explain the work cheaply—without inventing certainty, duplicating everything, or weakening privacy.**
